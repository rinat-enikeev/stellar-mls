Dear Editors,

I am submitting the enclosed manuscript, "Postmortems from a SNARK-Gated
Group Registry on Stellar," for consideration as a feature article in
IEEE Security & Privacy.

The article is an experience report on Onym, an alpha-stage Stellar
contract that anchors group-membership state as a 32-byte commitment and
gates each state transition on a Groth16 SNARK. The technical contribution
is not a new cryptographic primitive — it is two postmortems and a
design-review template derived from real engineering missteps that
happened during deployment. The first postmortem (P1) is an authorization
vulnerability in which a relation's soundness theorem was a true statement
about the wrong predicate; the second (P2) is a transport-layer
co-membership leak that lived in the Security Considerations bullet of a
companion document. A third demoted lesson concerns R1CS cost as the
binding constraint on host-function choice for primitives that straddle
the circuit boundary.

I believe the article fits the magazine's stated scope for the following
reasons. First, the lessons generalize beyond Onym to any SNARK-gated
contract: review topology, simulation-extractability vs. plain
knowledge-soundness, and identity-coupling antipatterns are recurring
issues across the applied-ZK design space, not Onym-specific. Second, the
piece is deliberately written as a builder's postmortem rather than a
research result — there are no new theorems and no performance tables
beyond the on-chain instruction count. Third, the submission is honest
about what it does not claim: the abstract states upfront that the
deployed verification keys are the output of a single-contributor Phase-2
trusted setup, which makes every formal authorization claim vacuous
against the setup operator until a multi-contributor MPC has been re-run.

Two things the editors should know:

(1) Use of generative AI. The Acknowledgments contain a "Use of generative
AI" note describing how I worked with Claude (Anthropic) to translate a
working system into writing that a cryptographer would read as sound.
The note distinguishes AI as tutor and reader from AI as text generator,
and the postmortem content is my own engineering work.

(2) Concurrent submissions and prior dissemination. The manuscript has
not been submitted to any other venue. The deployed contract source and
the postmortem version tags are public on GitHub
(https://github.com/rinat-enikeev/stellar-mls); no preprint exists.

The figures (Fig. 1: data and metadata flow; Fig. 2: P1 before/after) are
included inline in the manuscript and are also supplied as separate PDF
source files per the IEEE Magazine submission requirements.

Suggested reviewers and conflicts of interest: I have no academic
co-authors and no institutional affiliation that constrains reviewer
choice. I would defer to the editor on reviewer selection within the
applied-cryptography and metadata-private-messaging community.

Thank you for your consideration.
